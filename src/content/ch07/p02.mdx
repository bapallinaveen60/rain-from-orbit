That is the actual flow chart, colour-coded by module. Follow it from the top and every box is something you have already met:

- <b>Level 1 (echo power)</b> enters at the top; **PRE** turns it into <code>σ⁰<sub>m</sub></code> and <code>Z<sub>m</sub></code> and decides rain or no-rain.
- **VER** (green) takes the environmental grid and strips the non-precipitating attenuation, giving clean <code>Z<sub>m</sub></code>.
- **SRT** (pink) reads its own database to produce <code>PIA<sub>srt</sub></code>.
- **CSF** (blue) does type classification and BB detection — and feeds the <b>R–D<sub>m</sub></b> choice.

Two things in that diagram we have not met yet.

The **cylinders** are databases, not code: the SRT σ⁰ climatology and the DSD database holding the ε prior. They are updated offline, between processing runs, which is why a whole month must be processed as a unit.

And at the bottom right: **NUBF parameters**.

<Alert title="NON-UNIFORM BEAM FILLING!!!">

Core's footprint is about **5 km across**. Rain is not uniform over 5 km — a convective core three kilometres wide sits inside one footprint alongside much lighter rain.

Because reflectivity and attenuation are **non-linear** in rain rate, averaging over a non-uniform beam does not give the average rain rate. It biases the answer, and the bias grows with how lumpy the rain is.

<p style={{ marginBottom: 0 }}>That is what the NUBF correction in the diagram is for, and it is one of the reasons heavy convective rain remains the hardest case even with two frequencies.</p>

</Alert>
