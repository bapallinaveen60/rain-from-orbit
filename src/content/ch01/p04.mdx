<Box tone="a" step={11}>

The first spaceborne precipitation radar flew on **TRMM** in November 1997 and covered the tropics. Its successor, the **GPM Core Observatory**, launched from Tanegashima on **27 February 2014** — a joint NASA and JAXA mission.

It carries two instruments at once: **GMI**, a radiometer with a 904 km swath, and **DPR**, the Dual-frequency Precipitation Radar.

</Box>

<Box step={12}>

Now the orbit, which is a deliberate and slightly unusual choice:

- **65° inclination** — reaches well past the tropics into the mid-latitudes, where snow and shallow frontal rain live.
- **Non-sun-synchronous** — the crossing time drifts.
- <b>~93 minutes</b> per orbit, about sixteen orbits a day.

</Box>

<Box tone="d" span step={13}>

<Talk char="dot2">Why would you *want* the crossing time to drift? That sounds like sloppy engineering.</Talk>

<Talk char="core">Because a sun-synchronous orbit would see the tropics at 01:30 and 13:30 and *never any other time*. Convection over land peaks in the afternoon. Offshore convection peaks at night. I would have been permanently blind to the daily cycle of the thing I was built to measure.</Talk>

<Psst>Over a month, the drift lets GPM sample every hour of the day at every latitude it reaches. <b>SPLAT.</b></Psst>

</Box>

<Box tone="c" span>

<Note><b>NOTE:</b> In November 2023 the spacecraft was boosted from about 407 km to 442 km to slow fuel use. The footprint grew from 5.0 km to 5.5 km and the swath from 256 km to 278 km. It is expected to keep observing into about 2030.</Note>

</Box>
