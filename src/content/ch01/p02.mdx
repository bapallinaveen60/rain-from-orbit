<Talk char="dot2">Hang on, **Core**. Why is this hard? Put a bucket outside. Measure what's in the bucket. Rain measured!</Talk>

<Talk char="core">That works for the bucket, **Dot**. It tells you nothing about the next valley, and nothing at all about the ocean — which is seventy percent of the planet and has essentially no buckets.</Talk>

</Box>

<Box step={5}>

<b>The problem:</b> rain is both violently local and globally consequential. One thunderstorm cell three kilometres across can drop more water in an hour than the surrounding hundred kilometres gets in a week.

So this is not a measurement problem. It is a **sampling** problem — and the instruments are not where the water is.

</Box>

<Box tone="d" step={6}>

<b>A solution:</b> look down at it. But *how* you look down matters enormously, and there are two very different ways.

- A **radiometer** listens to the microwaves the atmosphere emits and scatters. Wide swath, whole globe, several times a day.
- A **radar** sends its own pulse and times the echo.

</Box>

<Box tone="c" step={7}>

The difference is everything, and it is the subject of the next page.
