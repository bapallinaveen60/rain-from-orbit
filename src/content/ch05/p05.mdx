Above about 1.5 mm it climbs steeply and behaves exactly as advertised. Below that, it does something awkward: it sags **negative**, bottoming out near −1.2 dB at D<sub>m</sub> ≈ 1.0 mm, before coming back up.

</Box>

<Box tone="c" step={11}>

<Talk char="dot2">Negative? Your Ka echo is *bigger* than your Ku echo? How?</Talk>

<Talk char="core">Mie scattering is not a smooth decline. Around that size there is a brief resonance where Ka backscatter is *enhanced* relative to Rayleigh. It only lasts a decibel or so, but it lands right where most of the world's rain is.</Talk>

</Box>

<Box tone="d" step={12}>

<b>Two consequences, both bad:</b>

- Every DFR value between about <b>−1.2 dB and 0</b> corresponds to **two** different drop sizes — one below 1 mm, one above.
- The curve is so **flat** there that a few tenths of a decibel of calibration error moves your answer by a large fraction of a millimetre.

<Psst>Light rain — most of the rain on Earth, and most of what falls over India during a break spell — lives in exactly that region.</Psst>

</Box>

<Box span>

<Alert title="TERMINOLOGY ALERT!!!">

**DFR** and <b>DFR<sub>m</sub></b> are not the same thing, and papers are not always careful about which they mean.

<p style={{ marginBottom: 0 }}><b>DFR<sub>m</sub></b> is the *measured* ratio — it contains both the scattering difference *and* all the accumulated differential attenuation from Chapter 4. **DFR** without the subscript usually means the *effective* or intrinsic ratio, after attenuation correction, which is the pure size gauge. The curve on this page is the effective one.</p>
